Play & Coach: what the coach is (and isn't)

You play pot-limit Big O at 5/10 against up to six bots, with 30, 50, 100 or 200bb stacks reset every hand. Preflop you can fold, limp/call or raise pot. Postflop you can bet or raise ½ pot or pot.

This is not a GTO solver. Nobody has solved 7-max pot-limit Big O hi-lo; it's far beyond what commercial solvers do even for 4-card PLO. Instead the coach finds the best response against the opponents you're actually playing, which is what wins money against humans:

  1. Opponent model. Each bot reads its own hand like a person does (how close its high is to the nuts, which nut low it holds, which nut low draw) and then plays by its profile (hover a player's type at the table to see it):
    • Fish: calling station. Never folds to a 3-bet preflop. Always calls with the nut or 2nd-nut low, any flush, a nut flush draw or a wrap; calls most other draws and two pair; weaker pairs and lows about half the time; folds hands with nothing or only backdoors. Raises only with the nuts.
    • Nit: continues only with near-nut highs, the nut low, or a nut draw with a real high; folds a 2nd-nut low without a good high; always bets the river nuts.
    • Maniac: always calls with any flush, calls any flush or straight draw, bets and raises any piece, bluffs a lot, but folds pure air to a real bet.
    • Reg: pot odds plus nut awareness; continues with nut lows, strong highs, nut flush draws, wraps and nut low draws; folds non-nut lows to heavy action and folds nothing.
    Nobody folds the nuts, and nobody continues against a bet of a third of the pot or more with nothing (no pair that matters, no low, no low draw, no flush draw, no real straight draw). Bots never play on just to reach a showdown; every decision comes from their profile.
  2. Hand reading. At each of your decisions, the coach deals your opponents thousands of possible hands, keeping the ones that fit what they've done so far. A Nit who re-potted preflop gets mostly premium hands, a Fish who limped gets almost anything.
  3. Rollouts. From every option you have (fold, call, pot), it plays the hand to the end against those hands, with the bots following their model and you continuing in the equity-driven Aggro style (below). The same cards are used for every option, so differences between options are measured precisely.
  4. Grade. EV is the chips you win from this point in big blinds (fold = 0). Your choice is rated Best, Good (within noise), Inaccuracy (<1.5bb lost), Mistake (<4bb) or Blunder.

Preflop is judged by playing the hand out. For each option (fold, limp or call, raise pot) the coach plays the rest of the hand thousands of times: opponents get hands that fit what they've done, everyone behind responds by their profile (folding, calling or re-raising), and you continue in the Aggro style. An earlier version estimated preflop EV from equity with position and table adjustments. Checked against hundreds of spots played out 600 times each, that formula over-rated 3-bets by about 6bb facing a raise (the table bonus gave weak hands credit for fish paying off) and under-rated opens by about 2bb. The play-out simulation was unbiased in both. Explain still shows your equity against the players in the pot and what happens after each option, plus your head-to-head equity against a premium A-2-3-Q-T double-suited and against a random hand.

Postflop, because the coach knows how each type plays, it recommends exploiting them: value-bet fish thinner, fold to nits' raises, call down maniacs lighter. Turn off "Show player types" to practice without reads. Deeper coach settings run more rollouts and shrink the ± ranges.

The Aggro playbook: aggressive, but only where the equity is real

The coach assumes you play this style on later streets, and Explain names the rule that fits your hand ("Playbook") and calls out equity swings since your last decision. It was built by playing the same deals twice, once with the solid-reg style and once with each candidate change, and keeping only changes that won money. Against a mixed table it wins about +116bb/100 more than the reg style. It also wins at every table type (tough +39, all-reg +24, all-nit +65, soft +173, wild +101), heads-up (+39) through 7-handed, and at every stack depth (30bb +12 before the short-stack adjustments, which add about +5 more; 200bb +235).

  • Bet strong hands pot-sized, every time. The nuts, near-nut highs, the nut low with a decent high, scoop hands. No slow-playing. These opponents pay off big bets: potting instead of half-potting strong hands is worth about +20bb/100 on its own, the biggest single rule (always betting them adds +5bb/100 more).
  • Raise the nuts and nut-low scoop hands whenever you face a bet (+3.6bb/100), and almost nothing else. Raising strong-but-not-nut highs lost 2–3bb/100, and raising because a hand has good equity against random hands (two pair on a straight board, a pair plus weak draws into two maniacs) cost about 14bb/100. Two-way nut draws can raise sometimes (+0.8bb/100).
  • Press when the card swings your way. If your equity jumped 8+ points since the last street and you now hold a strong high or the nut low, bet (+2bb/100 on top of the other rules).
  • Nut low draws keep going on the flop and turn, even facing a raise. With five hole cards they get there often, and when they do they hold the nut low. This was the biggest single leak in the reg style (+10bb/100).
  • With little behind, get it in. When your stack is under 2× the pot, raise strong hands and two-way nut draws instead of calling (+4.4bb/100). Don't widen it to medium hands (−2.8bb/100), except at 30bb: with short stacks, also get it in with medium-strong highs, and raising on general hand strength is fine. There's little left behind to lose (+5bb/100 at 30bb; no difference at 50bb, so it only applies to stacks of 40bb or less).
  • Bet the nut low; you can't lose that half. Heads-up, a strong (not nut) high is a value bet too.
  • Bet two-way nut draws (nut flush draw or wrap plus a nut low draw or a real high), usually ½ pot. Draws mostly make money by being called, so raising with them is only break-even.
  • Play the player.
    • Never bluff when a fish is in the pot (+12bb/100). They don't fold, so bet only when you're likely best. But don't value-bet medium hands into them either (−4bb/100): when a fish calls, it's usually with something.
    • Call maniacs down lighter (+3.6bb/100). They bet and bluff with far more than a reg.
    • Respect nits' and fish's bets, and fold more than the pot odds alone say. Calling fish bets lighter lost 2.3bb/100.
    • When only nits are left, bet, and size value bets at ½ pot. They fold far too often (+2bb/100), and pot-sized value bets just fold them out (½ pot was +1.3bb/100 better).
    • Trap maniacs. With a strong hand and a maniac still to act behind you, check and let them bet, then raise (+3.2bb/100, river included). If you do bet into a maniac, bet pot: smaller value bets to invite raises lost 3–5bb/100. Against everyone else, bet your strong hands yourself.
    • Don't stab just because it was checked to you (−2bb/100). These players check plenty of hands that call.
    • Fold more on the river: river bets from these players are mostly real (+1.9bb/100). Calling lighter on the turn or river lost 2.5–3.3bb/100.
  • Preflop, play more hands than a solid reg when the table is soft. Against regs and nits, play a reg's range. With fish and maniacs at the table, open up to about 24 percentile points looser (once roughly half the table is fish or maniacs), because your postflop edge pays for the extra hands. Doing this by table beat a fixed looser range by +12bb/100 at a mixed table and +11bb/100 at a table of regs. From early position mostly limp: in full play-outs limping beat raising at every hand strength. On the button when it's folded to you, raise. Don't 3-bet light (−2.5bb/100), not even against maniacs, and don't raise every hand you open (−3bb/100). Facing a raise, call wider against fish (+3.7bb/100) and a little wider against nits (+1bb/100; tightening up against nits' raises lost 2–4bb/100). Your postflop edge against both pays for it. Going much looser than that stops paying on an average table, but with fish and maniacs in the game the coach will tell you to limp even wider.
  • What doesn't work: betting or calling because a hand has good equity against random hands (a weak pair in a 5-way pot is behind the hands that call); thin value bets with medium highs heads-up (−2.7bb/100); calling with non-nut low draws; river bluffs with nothing (−1.8bb/100, because these players call with real hands and fold only air); and big bets with medium hands (keep those ½ pot).

Big O rules in one paragraph

Big O is Omaha with five hole cards. You must use exactly two hole cards and exactly three board cards for each half. It is usually played hi-lo: the best high hand takes half the pot, the best qualifying low takes the other half. A low needs five different ranks of 8 or lower; aces count low, and straights and flushes don't hurt a low (so A-2-3-4-5 is the best low). If no one qualifies for low, high takes the whole pot. Switch to High only at the top to train 5-card PLO.

What "equity" means here

Equity is the share of the pot you win on average if the hand is run to showdown with nobody folding. A scoop counts as 1.0, half the pot counts as 0.5, a quartered low (you split the low half with one other player) counts as 0.25, and so on. With N players, the "fair share" is 1/N; above that you're doing better than average.

How the Monte Carlo simulation works

Enumerating every runout and every possible opponent hand is far too slow in Big O (there are billions of combinations against several opponents). Instead the simulator deals at random, over and over:

  1. Take the cards that are known (your hand, any face-up hands, the board) out of the deck.
  2. Shuffle what's left and deal the unknown cards: random opponent hands and the rest of the board.
  3. For every player, check all 10 two-card hole combos × 10 three-card board combos (100 five-card hands) for the best high, and the same for the best qualifying low.
  4. Split the pot by the hi-lo rules and record your share.

The average share over many trials converges to your true equity. The standard error is σ/√n, so 4× more trials halves the error. At 20,000 trials the 95% interval is usually about ±0.5 to ±0.7 percentage points, which is plenty for training.

The Solver tab

The Solver finds game-theory-optimal (equilibrium) strategies for heads-up turn and river spots with counterfactual regret minimization (CFR+), the method commercial solvers use. Each player's range is a sample of real 5-card hands from the top X% of starting hands; hands that share a card never meet, which matters a lot in 5-card Omaha. Bets and raises are ½ pot or pot (re-raises pot), pot limit, up to 3 bets per street. Turn solves cover every river card (training samples 20 river cards at a time, which is unbiased; the final numbers use all of them).

Flop solves cover every turn and river. The flop and turn are solved at full card detail (a separate strategy for every turn card); inside a flop solve the river groups hands into strength levels by their share of the pot on each exact runout, because exact river strategies for all 2,352 runouts don't fit in memory, and turn and river raises use pot size only. When you pick a turn card, that turn and river are re-solved exactly (both raise sizes, every river card, a separate strategy per river) from the ranges the flop solution arrives with, so every turn and river strategy you look at is exact for the sampled hands. The flop result reports the exact exploitability of its flop and turn decisions, measured over every turn and river.

Exploitability tells you how close a solve is to perfect: it is how much a perfect opponent could gain against the solution, as a share of the pot. River solves typically finish around 0.1%, turn solves around 1–2% (Normal or Deep accuracy brings it down). The equilibrium is the unexploitable baseline; the Play & Coach tab instead shows the best play against the specific opponent types at your table.

When equities are exact

Whenever every hand is known (the Calculator with all hands entered, the Equity Quiz with face-up hands, and the Matchup), the trainer doesn't simulate at all: it checks every possible remaining board and gives the exact answer, even preflop (850,668 boards heads-up, a few seconds). Monte Carlo is used only when some hands are unknown (hidden quiz hands, random opponents, and the coach's reads of your opponents), where enumerating every possible hand isn't feasible.

The engine has been verified three ways: all 2,598,960 five-card hands produce exactly the 7,462 distinct high-hand values and the 56 distinct 8-or-better lows that poker math says they should; 1,000,000 random Omaha spots match a slow brute-force reference for both high and low (exactly two hole cards, exactly three board cards); and 100,000,000 Monte Carlo trials across 200 spots agree with exact enumeration within pure sampling noise.

Training tips for hi-lo Big O

  • Scooping is everything. Hands that only make one half (especially non-nut lows) win much less than they look.
  • A-2 plus A-3 or A-2 with a third low card gives counterfeit protection; a lone A-2 gets counterfeited often.
  • Double-suited hands with the suited ace and connected cards play best multiway.
  • Pairs, trips and three cards of one suit are weaker than they look: duplicate cards waste your own outs.
  • Preflop equities run close. A great hand vs a random hand is often only 60–65%. Postflop, the gaps get much bigger.
